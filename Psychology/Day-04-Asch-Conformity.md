## Going along with the crowd: Asch's conformity experiment

*About a 5-minute read. Answers to the recall questions are in the collapsible section at the bottom.*

---

**Swarthmore College, USA, 1951.** You've signed up for a "vision test." You walk into a small room and take the second-to-last seat at a table with seven other students. A man shows a card with one line on it, then a second card with three lines labelled A, B and C. The question: *which line matches the first one?*

It's embarrassingly easy. Round one: everyone gives the obvious answer, you included. Round two: same. You start to wonder why this needs a psychologist.

Round three. The answer is clearly **B**. The first student says, calmly, **"A."** The second says "A." The third, the fourth, the fifth, the sixth: "A," every one of them. Now everyone's turned to you.

What you don't know is that the other seven are actors, told to give the wrong answer on certain rounds. *You* are the experiment.

## The visual

```
  Reference          Comparison lines
     |               |        |        |
     |               |        |        |
     |                        |        |
     |                        |        |
                                       |
                     A        B        C      ← B matches

  Seats:  [A] [A] [A] [A] [A] [A] [YOU] [A]
          Six people before you say A. The right answer is B.
```

**What happened?** When people answered alone, they got it wrong less than 1% of the time. With the group giving wrong answers, people went along with the group on about **a third** of those rounds. And **3 out of 4 people** gave in at least once. Afterwards, many said they *knew* the group was wrong but didn't want to stand out. A few started to doubt their own eyes.

## What this means

We go along with a group for two different reasons:

1. **To be right:** "If everyone says A, maybe they see something I don't." Copying others is usually a good shortcut.
2. **To be liked:** "I know it's B, but I don't want to look stupid." That's social pressure.

**The hopeful part:** when Asch planted just **one actor who gave the right answer**, conformity dropped to about **5%**. You don't need a crowd on your side. **One ally** breaks the spell. Even letting people write their answer privately instead of saying it out loud cut conformity a lot.

**Where you see it today:** "10,000 people bought this," star ratings, "trending," laugh tracks on sitcoms, the meeting where nobody speaks up because nobody else did. Marketers call it **social proof**, and it's Asch's experiment at scale.

**Did it hold up?** Yes, though the effect is smaller in many modern repeats. A review of 133 studies in 17 countries found conformity has declined since the 1950s in the US, and that it's higher in cultures that value the group over the individual.

## Try it today

- **On yourself:** for one day, notice every time you choose something *because* others did: the "most popular" dish, the "bestseller" tag, the top-rated option, a reply you softened in a group chat. Count them. Then ask: would I have picked it alone?
- **With others (the fun, harmless version):** with 3 friends in on it, ask a fourth friend (who's happy to play a "quick guessing game") how many sweets are in a jar or how long a song clip lasted. Your 3 friends answer first, all with the same number, way too high. Does the fourth friend's guess drift up? Then repeat with one friend giving a sensible number. Tell them everything straight afterwards, and keep it light. The goal is a laugh, not embarrassment.
