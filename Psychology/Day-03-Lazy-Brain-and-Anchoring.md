## Your lazy brain: System 1, System 2 and anchoring

*About a 5-minute read. Answers to the recall questions are in the collapsible section at the bottom.*

---

Before anything else, answer this in your head. Fast. Don't overthink it.

> **A bat and a ball cost ₹110 in total. The bat costs ₹100 more than the ball. How much does the ball cost?**

Got a number? Hold on to it.

Most people instantly hear **₹10**. It *feels* right. But check it: if the ball is ₹10, the bat is ₹110, and together they cost ₹120. Wrong.

The real answer is **₹5** (ball ₹5, bat ₹105, total ₹110).

**Here's the story.** In 2005, the economist Shane Frederick gave this puzzle to thousands of students, including students at Harvard, MIT and Princeton. At those top universities, around **half** still said ₹10 (well, 10 cents). These weren't people who are bad at maths. Their brains simply answered *before checking*.

## What this means

Your mind has two modes:

- **System 1** is fast, automatic and effortless. It recognises faces, reads words, and blurts out "₹10." It runs your day.
- **System 2** is slow, careful and effortful. It does long division and double-checks things. But it's **lazy**: if System 1's answer *feels* fine, System 2 doesn't bother to check.

Most of the time this is a great deal. But it leaves a door open, and one of the easiest ways through it is a number.

## The visual: anchoring

In 1997, two German psychologists asked one group, "Did Gandhi die before or after age 9?", and another group, "Before or after age 140?" Both questions are obviously silly. Then each group guessed his actual age.

```
 0          50          100         150
 |-----------|-----------|-----------|
   9 ──────▶ 50                          "before or after 9?"  → guessed ~50
                  67 ◀─────────────── 140  "before or after 140?" → guessed ~67
                     ┆
                  real age 78
```

| Anchor in the question | Average guess | Real age |
|---|---|---|
| 9 | about 50 | 78 |
| 140 | about 67 | 78 |

**That's anchoring.** A number everyone *knew* was absurd still dragged their guesses towards it. System 1 grabs the first number it sees and adjusts from there, and it never adjusts far enough. System 2 doesn't step in, because the guess *feels* like your own.

**You see it everywhere:**

- "~~₹9,999~~ ₹4,999" makes ₹4,999 feel cheap.
- A salary negotiation where whoever names a number first sets the range.
- "Most people donate ₹500" on a fundraising page.

**Did it hold up?** Yes, very strongly. Both the bat-and-ball problem and anchoring have been repeated many times, including in large studies across dozens of labs. Anchoring is one of the most reliable effects in all of psychology.

## Try it today

- **On yourself:** next time you shop online, find one product with a "was ₹X, now ₹Y" price. Before reading the old price, cover it and decide what *you* would pay. Then look. Did the crossed-out price change how good the deal feels?
- **With others:** ask 6 people, one at a time, "Is the Eiffel Tower taller or shorter than **150 metres**? How tall is it?" Ask 3 of them that version and the other 3 the same question with **900 metres**. Compare the two groups' average guesses, then tell them it was an anchoring test. (The real height is about 330 m.)
