## Your memory is a liar: false memories

*About a 5-minute read. Answers to the recall questions are in the collapsible section at the bottom.*

---

**Seattle, 1974.** Forty-five students sit in a dark room watching short films of car crashes, the kind used in driving-safety classes. Nothing fancy. Afterwards, each student gets a questionnaire.

Here's the trick. Everyone saw the *same* films, but they didn't all get the same question. Some were asked:

> "About how fast were the cars going when they **hit** each other?"

Others got the same question with one word swapped: **smashed**, **collided**, **bumped** or **contacted**.

One word. Watch what it did to their answers.

## The visual

Average speed guessed after watching the same crash:

| Verb in the question | Average guess |
|---|---|
| smashed | **40.5 mph** |
| collided | 39.3 mph |
| bumped | 38.1 mph |
| hit | 34.0 mph |
| contacted | **31.8 mph** |

"Smashed" added almost 9 mph compared with "contacted." Same crash, different word.

**The twist came a week later.** Loftus ran a second version: new students, one crash film, and the same "smashed" or "hit" question. When those students came back a week later, they were asked: *"Did you see any broken glass?"* There was **no broken glass** in the film. About 1 in 3 people from the "smashed" group said yes, roughly double the "hit" group. The word didn't just change their guess. It **added glass to their memory.**

## What this means

Memory isn't a video recording. Each time you remember something, your brain *rebuilds* it, and whatever it picks up along the way (a word, a question, someone else's version) can get stitched in. You can't tell the difference afterwards. It just feels like remembering.

Loftus later pushed it much further. In the **"lost in the mall"** study (1995), she gave people a booklet of childhood stories supposedly collected from their family. Three were real. One was invented: getting lost in a shopping mall as a small child. About **1 in 4 people** came to "remember" the fake event, some adding details of their own.

**Did it hold up?** Yes. The word effect has been repeated many times. A careful 2023 repeat of the mall study found about **35%** of people formed a false memory, even more than the original. It's a big reason police are now trained not to ask leading questions.

## Try it today

- **On yourself:** pick a memory you're *certain* about, like a family story you've told many times. Ask someone else who was there to describe it before you tell your version. Note every detail where your versions differ. Who's right? (Probably neither of you, completely.)
- **With others:** show 4 friends the same busy photo for 10 seconds, then hide it. Ask 2 of them "Did you see **the** dog?" and the other 2 "Did you see **a** dog?" (with no dog in the photo). The word "the" assumes a dog was there. Count the yeses, then reveal the trick.

## Quick recall: Day 1

1. What was Pavlov actually studying when he stumbled onto conditioning?
2. What's the name for learning that links a signal to a feeling?
3. Why does your phone's buzz give you a little jolt before you've even looked?

<details><summary>Answers</summary>

1. Digestion (he won a Nobel Prize for it).
2. Classical conditioning.
3. The buzz has been paired with messages and good news so many times that the sound alone now triggers the excitement.
