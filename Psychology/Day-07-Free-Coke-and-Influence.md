## The free Coke: reciprocity and the 7 principles of influence

*About a 7-minute read, including the week recap. Answers are in the collapsible sections.*

---

**Cornell University, 1971.** You've signed up for an "art appreciation" study. You and another student, Joe, sit in a room rating paintings. During a short break, Joe slips out. He comes back with two bottles of Coke, and hands you one. *"I asked if I could get myself a Coke, and he said it was okay, so I got one for you, too."*

Small, friendly gesture. You thank him and get back to rating paintings.

After the study, Joe asks a favour. He's selling raffle tickets for a new car, 25 cents each, and he gets a prize if he sells the most. *"Any would help, the more the better."*

Joe is an actor, of course. Half the participants got a free Coke; half got nothing. The researcher, Dennis Regan, wanted to know one thing: **does a 10-cent Coke buy anything?**

It did. People given the Coke bought about **twice as many** raffle tickets, spending far more than the Coke was worth. And here's the creepy part: it **didn't matter much whether they liked Joe.** Even people who found him annoying felt the pull to pay him back.

## What this means

**Reciprocity** is one of the deepest rules in every human culture: *if someone gives you something, you owe them.* It helped our ancestors share food and build trust. But it can be **triggered on purpose**, with a gift you never asked for, to get something much bigger back.

The psychologist Robert Cialdini spent years undercover in sales teams, charities and marketing firms, and found **7 "yes buttons"**. Each has an honest use and a manipulative one:

| Button | How it's used against you | Your defence |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Reciprocity | Free samples, "free" trials, a favour before the ask | Accept gifts as gifts; you don't owe a purchase |
| Commitment | A small yes ("just sign up for the free webinar") before the big ask | Ask: "Would I say yes if this were the *first* request?" |
| Social proof | "10,000 sold today," fake reviews | Ask: "Are these people like me, and is it even true?" |
| Authority | Uniforms, titles, "doctor-recommended" | Ask: "Is this person an expert *in this exact thing*?" |
| Liking | Flattery, mirroring, "we have so much in common!" | Separate how you feel about the person from the deal |
| Scarcity | "Only 2 left!", countdown timers | Ask: "Do I want it for what it *is*, or because it's disappearing?" |
| Unity | "As a fellow Puneri / engineer / Indian..." | Shared identity is not a reason to trust an offer |

**Did it hold up?** Yes, broadly. Reciprocity, commitment, social proof and scarcity have been tested many times in labs and in the field. The Coke study itself was small, but the reciprocity effect has repeated in many forms, like restaurant mints raising tips and free address labels raising charity donations.

## Try it today

- **On yourself (influence hunt):** for the next 24 hours, screenshot every influence attempt you meet: ads, apps, shop signs, WhatsApp forwards, sales calls. Aim for 10. Label each with its button from the table. Which button shows up most?
- **With others:** with 6 friends who know it's a mini experiment, ask each for a small favour (for example, "Could you share my portfolio post?"). For 3 of them, give a small, genuine treat first, like a chocolate. Compare how many say yes, then tell them about the Coke study. Make the favour something they're happy to refuse.

# Week recap: 7 days, 7 ideas

1. **Pavlov's dogs:** we link signals to feelings. That's why your phone's buzz gives you a jolt.
2. **False memories:** one word ("smashed") can add broken glass to a memory.
3. **Your lazy brain:** System 1 answers first, and a silly number (age 9 or 140) still anchors your guess.
4. **Asch's lines:** we go along with a wrong crowd, but one ally breaks the spell.
5. **The shaky bridge:** the body reacts first and the brain labels it, so fear can be mistaken for attraction.
6. **Choice blindness:** we confidently defend choices we never made.
7. **The free Coke:** a small gift creates a big feeling of owing someone.
