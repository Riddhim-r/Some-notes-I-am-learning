### Every topic has the same 8 parts

1. **Big question:** the one thing this topic tries to answer.
2. **Key ideas:** 3–6 ideas in plain words.
3. **Classic experiments:** what was done, what was found, and whether it held up when others repeated it.
4. **Do on yourself:** a safe experiment with you as the subject.
5. **Do with others:** a small experiment with friends or family who know it's an experiment.
6. **Read / watch:** where the real learning happens.
7. **Manipulation lens:** how this knowledge gets used to manipulate people, and how to spot it.
8. **Notebook question:** one question to answer in your own words when you finish.

### The loop for every experiment

Run each experiment through 6 steps: **Story → Guess → Do → Measure → Compare → Explain.** Read the setup but not the result. Write down your guess. Run it. Record numbers. Then read the real result, and explain any difference between it and yours. Guessing before you know the answer is what makes the learning stick.

### A weekly rhythm (about 5–6 hours)

- **2 days:** read or watch the resources (about 2 hours).
- **1 day:** guess, then do the experiment on yourself (about 1 hour).
- **2–3 days:** run the experiment with others (about 1 hour).
- **Weekend:** write the notebook page (about 1 hour), plus 15 minutes re-reading older pages. That re-reading is spaced review (see Day 1).

### Five rules for doing experiments with others

1. They agree to it and know it's an experiment.
2. They can stop at any time.
3. No harm, fear or embarrassment.
4. If there was a small trick, tell them the truth straight afterwards.
5. Keep it private: call them "Friend A," never by name.

## Day 1: How to learn effectively

**The big idea:** you don't learn by putting information *in*. You learn by pulling it *out*. Every time you force your brain to remember something, the memory gets stronger. Re-reading feels productive, but it mostly builds familiarity, not memory.

### 7 techniques that are proven to work

1. **Active recall:** after reading, close the book and write down everything you remember. In a classic 2006 study, students who tested themselves remembered far more a week later than students who re-read the material, even though the re-readers *felt* more confident.
2. **Spaced repetition:** review after 1 day, then 3 days, then 1 week, then 1 month. Each review comes just as you're starting to forget, which is exactly when it works best.
3. **The Feynman technique:** explain the idea in simple words, as if to a 12-year-old. Wherever you get stuck or reach for jargon, that's the gap in your understanding.
4. **Elaboration:** keep asking "why?" and "how?", and connect each new idea to your own life. A fact tied to something you already know sticks much better than a loose one.
5. **Prediction (guess first):** guessing an answer before you learn it, even wrongly, makes the real answer stick better. That's why the experiment loop starts with a guess.
6. **Interleaving:** mix topics instead of doing one block at a time. It feels harder, but it builds stronger understanding.
7. **Sleep and breaks:** your brain files memories while you sleep. Work in focused blocks (25 minutes on, 5 off) and don't trade sleep for study.

### 4 traps to avoid

- **Re-reading and highlighting:** they feel productive but teach very little.
- **The illusion of knowing:** recognising something ("oh yes, I've seen this") is not the same as being able to explain it.
- **Multitasking:** your phone nearby reduces focus even when it's face down.
- **Cramming:** it works for tomorrow's test and is gone in a week.

### Do on yourself

Pick two short paragraphs from any article. Learn paragraph A by re-reading it 3 times. Learn paragraph B by reading it once, then closing it and writing what you remember, twice. Two days later, write down everything you remember from each. Compare how much you kept.

### Do with others

Teach a friend one idea from today in 5 minutes, without notes. Afterwards, write down where you stumbled or said "umm." Those are your gaps.

### Read / watch

- **Book:** *Make It Stick* by Brown, Roediger and McDaniel. It covers the science behind every technique above.
- **Tool:** Anki, a free flashcard app that runs spaced repetition for you. Make 5–10 cards per topic.
- **Website:** retrievalpractice.org, with short practical guides on active recall.

## Day 2: How to read research and spot bad science

**The big idea:** one study proves very little. A finding becomes believable when it's big, clean, and repeated by other people. Your job as a reader is to ask questions, not to collect facts.

### How a paper is built

- **Abstract:** the whole study in one paragraph.
- **Introduction:** why they asked the question.
- **Method:** exactly what they did and to whom. This is the most important part.
- **Results:** the numbers.
- **Discussion:** what they *think* it means, which is their opinion, not the data.

**Reading order:** abstract, then the figures and tables, then the method, then the discussion. Skip the heavy statistics on the first read. On a second pass, come back for the details.

### The 8-question trust checklist

1. **How many people?** 20 people is weak. Hundreds is better.
2. **Who were they?** Most classic studies used American college students. Would the result hold for Indian office workers or grandparents?
3. **Was there a comparison group?** Without one, you can't tell whether the treatment did anything.
4. **Does it show cause, or just a link?** Ice cream sales and drownings both rise in summer, but ice cream doesn't cause drowning. Only a real experiment, where people are randomly put into groups, can show cause.
5. **How big is the effect?** "Statistically significant" only means the result probably isn't pure luck. It does *not* mean the effect is big or important.
6. **Has anyone repeated it?** Search "\[effect name\] replication." This is the single most useful habit you can build.
7. **Did they announce their plan in advance (pre-registration)?** Researchers who write down their hypothesis before collecting data can't quietly change it afterwards.
8. **Who paid for it?** A study funded by a company selling the product needs extra checking.

### Red flags in news headlines

- "Scientists prove..." (science rarely proves anything).
- A single study presented as settled.
- A study done on mice, reported as if it were about humans.
- A headline that says more than the paper does.

### Finding the original paper

Search Google Scholar for the title. Free versions are often on PsyArXiv or the author's own website, and the free Unpaywall browser extension finds legal free copies. You can also email the author: researchers almost always send a copy happily.

### Do on yourself

Find one "a new study shows..." psychology article in the news. Trace it back to the original paper and run the 8 questions on it. Give it a trust score from 1 to 5, and write one line on why.

### Do with others

Show a friend only the headline and ask, "How much do you believe this, from 1 to 10?" Then walk them through the 8 questions together and ask again. Note how much their score changed and which question changed it.

## Day 3: How to do research — designing a fair experiment

**The big idea:** a fair experiment changes *one* thing, keeps everything else the same, and measures what happens. Most bad experiments break that rule without noticing.

### The 8 steps

1. **Question:** "Does background music affect memory?"
2. **Hypothesis:** a specific, testable prediction. "People will remember fewer words with lyrics playing than in silence."
3. **Variables:**
   - **Independent:** the thing you change (music or silence).
   - **Dependent:** the thing you measure (the number of words remembered).
   - **Controlled:** everything you keep the same (list length, study time, time of day).
4. **Groups:** either compare different people (some get music, some get silence) or the same people under both conditions. With different people, assign them *randomly*, for example with a coin flip, so the groups are fair.
5. **Remove bias:**
   - **Counterbalancing:** if everyone does music first, tiredness or practice can mess up the result. Have half do music first and half do silence first.
   - **Demand characteristics:** people often act how they think you want them to. Don't reveal your hypothesis until the end.
   - **Experimenter effect:** you can nudge results without meaning to. A horse called Clever Hans "did maths" by reading his trainer's tiny body movements. Use the same script for everyone.
   - **Placebo and blinding:** where possible, people shouldn't know which condition they're in.
6. **Decide your measure in advance:** "memory" is vague, but "the number of words written down in 60 seconds" is clear.
7. **Write your plan down before you start:** your hypothesis, measure and number of people. This is pre-registration on a small scale, and it stops you fooling yourself afterwards.
8. **Analyse and conclude honestly:** compare averages, look at how spread out the scores are, and list the limits ("only 5 people, all friends").

### Do on yourself

Run the music experiment on yourself over 6 short sessions on different days, alternating music and silence. In each session, study 15 random words for 1 minute and write down as many as you can in 1 minute. Write your plan before session 1.

### Do with others

Run the same experiment with 4 friends. Two do music first, two do silence first. Use the same instructions, word for word, for everyone. Compare their averages with your own results.

### Read / watch

- **Free textbook:** *Research Methods in Psychology* by Price, Jhangiani and Chiang (open textbook). Read chapters 1, 5 and 6.
- **Free textbook:** Noba Project, "Research Designs" module. It's short and clear.
- **Video:** search "Clever Hans horse." Several short videos tell the story, and it's the best one-story lesson on experimenter bias.

## Day 4: Cognitive basics — how thinking works and fails

**The big idea:** your brain runs on two modes. **System 1** is fast, automatic and emotional, and it handles most of your day. **System 2** is slow, careful and effortful, and it's lazy: it lets System 1 decide whenever it can. Almost all manipulation works by keeping you in System 1.

### How memory and attention work

- **Attention is a narrow spotlight.** You can only focus on a little at a time, and whatever falls outside the spotlight is often not seen at all (the invisible gorilla).
- **Working memory is tiny.** It holds about 4 chunks at once, which is why a phone number is split into groups.
- **Long-term memory is rebuilt every time you recall it.** It's not a recording, which is why it can be changed (the "smashed" vs "hit" study).

### 10 biases worth knowing by heart

| Bias | In one line | How it gets used |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Confirmation bias | We notice what agrees with us and ignore what doesn't | Echo chambers, telling people what they already believe |
| Anchoring | The first number we see pulls our estimate towards it | "Was ₹9,999, now ₹4,999" |
| Availability | What comes to mind easily feels more common | Scary news makes rare dangers feel likely |
| Framing | The same fact feels different when worded differently | "95% fat-free" vs "5% fat" |
| Loss aversion | Losing hurts about twice as much as gaining feels good | "Don't miss out," free trials that are hard to cancel |
| Sunk cost | We keep going because of what we've already spent | Staying in bad deals and bad relationships |
| Halo effect | One good trait makes us assume the others are good too | Attractive or famous spokespeople |
| Bandwagon | We copy what most people do | "10,000 people bought this today" |
| Authority bias | We trust titles and uniforms | "Doctor-recommended" |
| Hindsight bias | After the fact, we think "I knew it all along" | Overconfident "experts" and gurus |

### Classic experiments

- **The bat-and-ball problem (2005):** "A bat and a ball cost ₹110 in total. The bat costs ₹100 more than the ball. How much is the ball?" Most people say ₹10. The answer is ₹5. System 1 answers before System 2 checks. *Held up?* Yes, very reliably.
- **Gandhi's age (1997):** people asked "Did Gandhi die before or after age 9?" guessed a much lower age than people asked "before or after age 140?", even though both anchors are obviously absurd. *Held up?* Yes. Anchoring is one of the strongest effects in psychology.

### Do on yourself

Keep a bias diary for 3 days. Each time you catch a bias in yourself (a sale that pulled you in, an opinion you only half-checked), write down the moment and name the bias from the table.

### Do with others

Run the Gandhi anchoring experiment with 6 people, one at a time. Ask 3 of them, "Did Gandhi die before or after age 9? Then guess his exact age." Ask the other 3 the same with "age 140." Compare the two averages, then tell everyone what you were testing. (The real answer is 78.)

### Manipulation lens

Most manipulation is a bias plus pressure. Time pressure, strong emotion and tiredness all switch off System 2. The defence is simple to say and hard to do: slow down, and ask "what would I decide if I had a day to think?"

### Read / watch

- **Book:** *Thinking, Fast and Slow* by Daniel Kahneman, Parts 1 and 2. Skip or distrust the chapter on "priming": Kahneman himself later admitted those studies didn't hold up.
- **Book (lighter):** *The Art of Thinking Clearly* by Rolf Dobelli, with one bias per 3-page chapter. It's good for daily reading.
- **Website:** The Decision Lab's bias index (thedecisionlab.com), with short explanations of more than 100 biases.
- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology's episode on cognition (YouTube).

## Day 5: Emotion basics — how feelings steer decisions

**The big idea:** emotions aren't the enemy of reason. They're fast signals that tell you what matters and get your body ready to act. But strong emotion shrinks attention and switches off careful thinking, and that's the door manipulators use.

### Key ideas

- **You need emotions to decide.** The neuroscientist Antonio Damasio studied a patient whose brain damage left his logic intact but removed his emotions. He couldn't choose between two lunch dates without endless reasoning. Feelings act as shortcuts for value.
- **Body first, label second.** Your body reacts (racing heart), and your brain then *labels* the feeling based on the situation. The same racing heart can be read as fear, excitement or attraction.
- **Arousal narrows focus.** When you're scared or excited, you see less and think less carefully.
- **Mood colours judgement.** In a bad mood, everything looks worse, including things that have nothing to do with the mood.
- **Naming a feeling calms it.** Saying "I'm feeling anxious" lowers its intensity. This is called affect labelling, and it's backed by brain-scan studies.
- **Emotions are partly learned.** Psychologists still argue about whether there are a few universal "basic" emotions, or whether the brain builds emotions from experience and culture.

### Classic experiments

- **The shaky bridge (1974):** men who met a woman on a scary, swaying bridge were more likely to call her afterwards than men who met her on a stable bridge. They mistook their fear for attraction. *Held up?* It's a famous small study. The general idea of mislabelling arousal has support, but the exact result is not rock-solid.
- **The pen-in-mouth study (1988):** holding a pen in your teeth (which forces a smile) made cartoons seem funnier. *Held up?* A large 2016 replication failed. A 2022 study across many countries found a small effect. It's a good example of how messy the science can be.

### Do on yourself

For 5 days, log your emotions 3 times a day: the emotion, its intensity from 1 to 10, what triggered it, and any decision you made while feeling it. When you feel something strong, try affect labelling: name the feeling out loud, then re-rate it after a minute.

### Do with others

Ask 6 friends to rate their overall life satisfaction from 1 to 10. Before asking, have 3 of them describe their favourite memory, and the other 3 describe their daily commute. Compare the two groups' averages, then explain what you were testing. Does a 2-minute mood shift change a "whole life" rating?

### Manipulation lens

Scams and pressure tactics start with emotion: panic ("your account will be blocked in 1 hour"), guilt ("after everything I've done for you"), flattery, or fear of missing out. The defence is to notice the feeling, name it, and refuse to decide while you're feeling it.

### Read / watch

- **Talk:** Lisa Feldman Barrett, "You aren't at the mercy of your emotions" (TED, about 18 minutes).
- **Book:** *How Emotions Are Made* by Lisa Feldman Barrett, for the modern view of emotion.
- **Book:** *Descartes' Error* by Antonio Damasio, on why emotion is needed for reason.
- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology's episodes on emotion (YouTube).

### Notebook question

## Day 6: Talking to AI as a learning partner

**The big idea:** AI answers the question you *asked*, not the one you *meant*, and it tends to agree with you. So the biases from Day 4 apply here too: a leading question gets a leading answer. This day is a short version of the full module in Phase 5, so you can use AI well for the rest of the curriculum.

### 7 rules

1. **Give context:** who you are, what you already know, and what you're trying to do.
2. **Say exactly what you want back:** the format, the length, and the level ("simple words, one everyday example").
3. **Make it quiz you, not lecture you:** that turns AI into an active-recall machine (Day 1).
4. **Ask neutral questions:** "What's the evidence for and against X?" instead of "Isn't X true?"
5. **Check sources yourself:** AI can confidently invent studies and citations. Look up anything that matters.
6. **Go step by step:** break big asks into small ones, and improve the answer in rounds.
7. **Let it ask you first:** "Before answering, ask me any questions you need."

### 4 prompts to reuse throughout this curriculum

```markdown
TUTOR
I'm learning [topic]. I already know [what you know]. Explain [concept] in simple words with one everyday example. Then ask me 3 questions to check I understood. Don't give the answers until I reply.

EXPERIMENT REVIEW
Here's my experiment plan: [plan]. Act as a strict reviewer. What could bias the result, what am I not controlling, and how would you fix each problem?

REPLICATION CHECK
Has [effect] held up in replications? Separate what's well established from what's disputed, name the key studies, and tell me how confident you are.

FEYNMAN CHECK
I'll explain [concept] in my own words. Point out anything wrong, missing or vague. Don't rewrite it for me.
[your explanation]
```

### Do on yourself

Ask an AI the same question two ways. First the leading version: "Isn't it true that power posing makes you more confident?" Then the neutral version: "What does research say about power posing?" Compare the two answers. Which one was more honest?

### Do with others

Give 3 friends the same goal, for example "get the AI to plan a 3-day Goa trip on a ₹15,000 budget," and have each write their own prompt. Run all three, then compare the results side by side with them. What made the best prompt best?

### Read / watch

- **Guide:** Anthropic's prompt engineering overview at docs.claude.com. It's practical and written for beginners.
- **Tutorial:** Anthropic's free interactive prompt engineering tutorial on GitHub.
- **Book:** *Co-Intelligence* by Ethan Mollick, on working with AI as a partner. His newsletter *One Useful Thing* is free.

### Notebook question

What's one way AI could "manipulate" a learner without meaning to, and how will you guard against it?

## Phase 1: Behavioural psychology and consciousness (4 weeks)

Phase 1 covers how behaviour is trained and how much of your mind runs without you. These two ideas sit under almost every manipulation technique you'll meet later.

### 1. Behavioural psychology (weeks 1–2)

**Big question:** how do rewards, punishments and repetition shape what we do?

**Key ideas**

- **Classical conditioning:** we link things that happen together, like Pavlov's bell and food, or your phone's buzz and excitement.
- **Operant conditioning:** behaviour followed by a reward happens more, and behaviour followed by punishment happens less.
- **"Negative reinforcement" is not punishment.** It means taking away something unpleasant (a seatbelt alarm stops when you buckle up), and it *increases* the behaviour.
- **Reward schedules:** rewards that come unpredictably (a "variable ratio" schedule) create the strongest, hardest-to-stop behaviour. That's how slot machines and social media feeds work.
- **The extinction burst:** when a reward stops, the behaviour first gets *worse* before it fades, like pressing a broken lift button harder.
- **Shaping:** you build a complex behaviour by rewarding small steps towards it.
- **Learned helplessness:** after repeated situations they can't control, people (and animals) stop trying, even when escape becomes possible.

**Classic experiments**

- **Pavlov's dogs (about 1900):** learned associations. *Held up?* Yes, it's rock-solid.
- **Thorndike's puzzle boxes (1898):** cats learned to escape faster by trial and error. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Skinner's boxes (1930s–50s):** rats and pigeons pressing levers under different reward schedules. *Held up?* Yes, it's the basis of modern behaviour therapy and app design.
- **Tolman's rats (1930):** rats explored a maze with no reward, then raced through it once food was added. They'd been learning all along. *Held up?* Yes, and it showed that learning isn't only about rewards.
- **Little Albert (1920):** fear conditioning in a baby. *Held up?* It was never properly replicated, and it's an ethics lesson more than a finding.
- **Learned helplessness (1967):** dogs given shocks they couldn't escape stopped trying to escape later. *Held up?* Yes, but in 2016 the original researchers reversed the explanation: giving up is the brain's default, and *control* is what has to be learned. The experiment itself would not pass ethics review today.

**Do on yourself:** pick one habit you want, like 20 minutes of reading. Attach a small, immediate reward and track it for 14 days. In parallel, count your phone pickups for 3 days and note which apps gave you an unpredictable reward.

**Do with others: the clapping game (shaping).** One friend leaves the room while the rest of the group picks a target action, for example "touch the window." When the friend comes back, nobody speaks. The group only claps louder as they get closer to the target. Time how long it takes, and talk afterwards about how it felt to be "trained."

**Manipulation lens:** unpredictable rewards power gambling, loot boxes, infinite feeds, and toxic relationships where someone is kind one day and cold the next. That unpredictability creates a powerful bond that's hard to break. The defence is to notice when you're chasing an unpredictable reward and to set rules in advance (time limits, spending limits).

**Read / watch**

- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology #11, "How to Train a Brain" (YouTube).
- **Video:** B. F. Skinner's pigeons playing ping-pong (search YouTube; under 2 minutes).
- **Book (practical):** *Atomic Habits* by James Clear, which is behavioural psychology applied to your own habits.
- **Book (manipulation lens):** *Hooked* by Nir Eyal. It's written for app builders, so read it as a manual of how apps hook you.

**Notebook question:** which unpredictable reward has the strongest hold on you, and what rule would weaken it?

### 2. Consciousness (weeks 3–4)

**Big question:** what is awareness, and how much of your mind runs without you knowing?

**Key ideas**

- **Most of your mind is unconscious.** You're aware of the results of thinking, not the process.
- **Attention is the gate:** what you don't attend to, you mostly don't experience.
- **Sleep works in cycles of about 90 minutes,** moving between deep sleep and REM (dreaming) sleep. Both help store memories.
- **Nobody knows for sure why we dream.** Theories include random brain activity, practising for threats, and sorting memories.
- **Hypnosis is real, but it isn't mind control.** It's focused attention plus high suggestibility, and people vary a lot in how hypnotisable they are.
- **The "hard problem":** why does any of this brain activity *feel* like something? This is still unsolved.

**Classic experiments**

- **Libet's free-will experiment (1983):** brain activity started before people felt they had decided to move. *Held up?* The timing is real, but a 2012 study suggests the early activity may be random background noise rather than a hidden decision. The free-will question is still open.
- **Split-brain patients (1960s onward):** when the two halves of the brain were disconnected, the talking half confidently invented reasons for actions the other half had caused. *Held up?* Yes. It shows we often make up explanations for our own behaviour.
- **Choice blindness (2005):** people chose the more attractive of two faces, the researchers secretly swapped the photos, and most people then defended a choice they never made. *Held up?* Yes, replicated with tastes, smells and even political opinions.
- **Randy Gardner (1964):** a 17-year-old stayed awake for 11 days, with growing problems in mood, memory and perception. *Held up?* Sleep-loss effects are well established. Never try it.

**Do on yourself:** keep a sleep and dream journal for 14 days. Record bedtime, wake time, sleep quality (1–10), any dreams you remember, and your mood and focus the next day (1–10). At the end, look for patterns.

**Do with others: the choice-blindness survey.** Ask a friend to rate 5 opinion statements, such as "Social media does more harm than good," from 1 to 10 on a paper sheet. While collecting the sheet, quietly reverse one answer (change 8 into 3) using a sticky note or a second copy. Then ask them to explain their reasoning for that answer. Does the friend defend the answer they never gave? Tell them the trick straight away.

**Manipulation lens:** choice blindness shows that people can be led to defend choices they never made. Sleep deprivation is used in harsh interrogations and by cults to weaken resistance. Watch out for myths, too: the famous 1957 "Eat popcorn" subliminal advertising study was faked.

**Read / watch**

- **Talk:** Anil Seth, "Your brain hallucinates your conscious reality" (TED, about 17 minutes).
- **Book:** *Being You* by Anil Seth.
- **Book (short):** *Consciousness: A Very Short Introduction* by Susan Blackmore.
- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology episodes on consciousness, sleep and altered states (YouTube).

**Notebook question:** after the choice-blindness survey, how sure are you that *your* reasons are the real reasons for your choices?

## Phase 2: People and groups (8 weeks)

Phase 2 is the heart of the manipulation thread. Most influence techniques come from social psychology, personality and relationships.

### 3. Social psychology (weeks 5–7)

**Big question:** how do other people change what we think and do?

**Key ideas**

- **Conformity:** we go along with a group either to be right (we assume they know) or to be liked (we don't want to stand out).
- **Obedience:** ordinary people follow authority much further than they expect to.
- **The bystander effect:** in a crowd, everyone waits for someone else to act.
- **Cognitive dissonance:** when our actions and beliefs clash, we often change the *belief* to fit what we did.
- **The fundamental attribution error:** we blame other people's character ("he's lazy") but blame the situation for ourselves ("I was tired").
- **In-group bias:** even random, meaningless groups make us favour "our side."
- **Cialdini's 7 principles of influence:** reciprocity, commitment, social proof, authority, liking, scarcity and unity.

**Classic experiments**

- **Asch's lines (1951):** about a third of answers went along with a wrong group. *Held up?* Yes, though the effect is smaller today and varies by culture.
- **Milgram's obedience study (1963):** 65% gave the maximum "shock." *Held up?* A partial repeat in 2009 found similar obedience up to a lower level. Later archive research showed that experimenters sometimes went off-script, so the 65% figure is debated.
- **Bystanders (1968):** people were slower to help in a "seizure" emergency when they thought others were present. *Held up?* In the lab, yes. But a 2019 study of real CCTV footage from 3 countries found that someone intervened in about 9 out of 10 public fights.
- **$1 vs $20 (1959):** people paid $1 to lie that a boring task was fun later rated it as more enjoyable than people paid $20. *Held up?* The general dissonance idea holds, though some specific findings are shakier.
- **Minimal groups (1971):** boys split into groups by a meaningless preference for one painter over another still favoured their own group. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Foot-in-the-door (1966):** agreeing to a small request made people much more likely to agree to a big one. *Held up?* Yes, with a modest effect.
- **The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971).** *Held up?* No. The guards were coached, so treat it as a warning about bad science.

**Do on yourself:** spend one week on an influence hunt. Collect 10 real examples (screenshots of ads, apps, sales messages, office situations) and label each with its Cialdini principle.

**Do with others: foot-in-the-door.** You'll need a big favour you genuinely want, for example "give feedback on my 10-page portfolio copy." Ask 6 friends. For 3 of them, ask a small favour first ("read this one paragraph") a day earlier. Compare how many said yes in each group, then tell everyone about the experiment.

**Manipulation lens:** each Cialdini principle has a dark version. Multi-level marketing schemes and cults climb a "commitment ladder" of small yeses, use fake social proof, and lean on authority. The defence is to ask, "Would I say yes to this request if it were the *first* thing they'd asked?"

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *Influence* by Robert Cialdini (new and expanded edition). This is the core book of your manipulation thread.
- **Book:** *The Social Animal* by Elliot Aronson, a classic and readable textbook.
- **Book (critical view):** *Behind the Shock Machine* by Gina Perry, on what really happened in Milgram's lab.
- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology's social psychology episodes (YouTube).

**Notebook question:** which of the 7 principles works best on you, and why?

### 4. Personality (weeks 8–9)

**Big question:** why are people different, and can we measure it?

**Key ideas**

- **The Big Five (OCEAN):** openness, conscientiousness, extraversion, agreeableness and neuroticism. This is the most scientifically supported model.
- **Traits are fairly stable but change slowly.** Most people become more conscientious and agreeable with age.
- **Genes explain roughly 40–50%** of the differences between people, according to twin studies.
- **The MBTI ("INTJ" and so on) is popular but weak science.** It forces people into boxes, and many people get a different type when they retake it.
- **The HEXACO model** adds a sixth trait, honesty-humility. Low honesty-humility strongly predicts manipulative behaviour.
- **The Dark Triad:** narcissism, Machiavellianism and psychopathy. Adding sadism makes the Dark Tetrad.

**Classic experiments**

- **The Forer effect (1949):** students all received the *same* vague "personal" description and rated it 4.3 out of 5 for accuracy. *Held up?* Yes, and it's why horoscopes feel accurate.
- **The marshmallow test (1970s):** children who waited longer for a second treat seemed to do better in life. *Held up?* A 2018 repeat found a much smaller link, largely explained by family background.
- **Twin studies (1980s onward):** identical twins raised apart turned out strikingly similar. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Big Five predictions (2019):** about 87% of claimed links between traits and life outcomes replicated. *Held up?* Yes, mostly, though the effects were often smaller.

**Do on yourself:** before taking any test, write down how you'd rate yourself on each of the Big Five. Then take the free IPIP-NEO (Big Five), the HEXACO test at hexaco.org, and the Short Dark Triad (SD3), and compare the results with your predictions.

**Do with others: the Forer demo.** Have 5 friends fill in a quick "personality quiz" (any 10 questions). Give each of them the *same* generic description ("You have a need for others to like you, yet you're critical of yourself..."), presented as their personal result. Ask them to rate its accuracy from 1 to 5, then reveal that everyone got the same text. *Bonus:* ask a close friend to rate you on the Big Five and compare it with your own rating.

**Manipulation lens:** fortune tellers and "psychics" use Forer-style statements and cold reading. Personality data has been used to target political ads. People high in Dark Triad traits often start with charm and then exploit. The defence is to notice when a description is true of almost everyone.

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *Personality: What Makes You the Way You Are* by Daniel Nettle.
- **Book:** *Without Conscience* by Robert Hare, on psychopathy from the man who built the standard test for it.
- **Book (fun, journalistic):** *The Psychopath Test* by Jon Ronson.
- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology's personality episodes (YouTube).

**Notebook question:** where did your test results disagree with how you see yourself, and who's more likely to be right?

### 5. Relationships (weeks 10–11)

**Big question:** why do we attract, bond and break apart?

**Key ideas**

- **Proximity and repeated exposure:** we tend to like the people (and things) we see often.
- **Similarity:** we're drawn to people like us.
- **Reciprocal liking:** we like people who like us.
- **Attachment styles:** secure, anxious or avoidant. They start in childhood and show up in adult relationships.
- **Gottman's "four horsemen":** criticism, contempt, defensiveness and stonewalling. Contempt is the strongest predictor of breakups.
- **The 5:1 ratio:** stable couples have about 5 positive interactions for every negative one.

**Classic experiments**

- **Mere exposure (1968):** repeated exposure to words, faces or symbols increased liking for them. *Held up?* Yes.
- **The 36 questions (1997):** strangers who asked each other increasingly personal questions felt much closer after 45 minutes. *Held up?* Yes, for closeness. It doesn't guarantee love, despite the headlines.
- **The Westgate housing study (1950):** friendships in a student housing block formed mostly between next-door neighbours. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Gottman's "love lab" (1990s):** couples' arguments predicted divorce. *Held up?* The patterns are useful, but the famous "90% accuracy" claim has been criticised as overstated.

**Do on yourself:** take the ECR-R attachment questionnaire (free online). For one week, also count your positive and negative interactions with the person closest to you, and work out your ratio.

**Do with others: the 36 questions.** With a willing friend, both of you rate how close you feel from 1 to 7 before you start. Go through the 36 questions together (about 45 minutes), then rate again afterwards.

**Manipulation lens:**

- **Love bombing** overloads reciprocity and attachment.
- **Hot-and-cold behaviour** is intermittent reinforcement (Phase 1).
- **Isolation** cuts someone off from the friends who would warn them.
- **Triangulation** brings in a third person to create jealousy.
- **Gaslighting** makes someone doubt their own memory.

Knowing what healthy patterns look like is what lets you spot unhealthy ones.

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *Attached* by Amir Levine and Rachel Heller, on attachment styles in adults.
- **Book:** *The Seven Principles for Making Marriage Work* by John Gottman.
- **Book:** *The Gaslight Effect* by Robin Stern.
- **Article:** "To Fall in Love With Anyone, Do This" by Mandy Len Catron (New York Times, 2015), which includes the 36 questions.

**Notebook question:** which manipulation tactic in relationships would be hardest for *you* to spot, and why?

### 6. Cultural psychology (week 12)

**Big question:** how much of "human nature" is really culture?

**Key ideas**

- **The WEIRD problem:** most psychology research was done on people from Western, Educated, Industrialised, Rich, Democratic societies, who are a small and unusual slice of humanity.
- **Individualist vs collectivist cultures:** "I" vs "we" ways of seeing yourself.
- **Analytic vs holistic thinking:** some cultures focus on the main object, others on the context and relationships around it.
- **Tight vs loose cultures:** these differ in how strict social rules are and how strongly they're enforced.

**Classic experiments**

- **The fish tank (2001):** shown the same aquarium scene, American participants described the big fish first and Japanese participants described the background and context. *Held up?* Yes, broadly.
- **The Müller-Lyer illusion across cultures (1966):** people in some societies were far less fooled by this famous line illusion. *Held up?* Yes, though the explanation is debated.
- **Conformity across cultures (1996):** a review of 133 Asch-style studies found more conformity in collectivist cultures. *Held up?* Yes.
- **The ultimatum game in 15 small societies (2001):** what counted as a "fair" split of money varied hugely between cultures. *Held up?* Yes.

**Do on yourself:** do the "Twenty Statements Test." Write 20 sentences starting "I am...", then count how many describe personal traits ("I am curious") and how many describe roles and groups ("I am a son," "I am an engineer").

**Do with others:** give the same test to people of different generations, such as parents and friends, and compare their ratios. Then show each person a busy photo for 10 seconds and ask them to describe it. Count how many details are about the main object and how many are about the background.

**Manipulation lens:** propaganda and marketing tune their message to culture, appealing to family honour in one place and personal achievement in another. The "unity" principle ("people like us") is especially powerful in tight-knit, collectivist settings.

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *The Geography of Thought* by Richard Nisbett.
- **Book:** *The WEIRDest People in the World* by Joseph Henrich.
- **Book:** *Rule Makers, Rule Breakers* by Michele Gelfand, on tight vs loose cultures.

**Notebook question:** which belief of yours that feels like "human nature" might actually be your culture?

## Phase 3: Problems and healing (8 weeks)

Phase 3 covers what goes wrong in the mind, how people recover, and what helps them thrive. Manipulators often target people at their lowest points, so understanding those points is part of the defence.

### 7. Clinical psychology (weeks 13–15)

**Big question:** what counts as a mental disorder, and who decides?

**Key ideas**

- **The 4 Ds:** psychologists look for deviance (unusual), distress (painful), dysfunction (gets in the way of life) and danger. No single one is enough on its own.
- **The manuals:** the DSM-5-TR (American) and ICD-11 (WHO) define disorders. They're agreements between experts that get revised over time, not laws of nature.
- **Most conditions are spectrums,** not on/off boxes.
- **Vulnerability plus stress:** genes and early life create a vulnerability, and stress triggers the problem.
- **The main groups:** anxiety, mood (depression, bipolar), OCD, trauma (PTSD), psychotic (schizophrenia), personality disorders (narcissistic, antisocial, borderline), and neurodevelopmental conditions (ADHD, autism).

**Classic experiments and studies**

- **"On Being Sane in Insane Places" (1973):** healthy people faked one symptom, got admitted to psychiatric hospitals, and were kept for weeks. *Held up?* A journalist's investigation (*The Great Pretender*, 2019) found serious problems, and parts may have been made up. It's a famous study to read critically.
- **The Adverse Childhood Experiences study (1998):** difficult childhood experiences were strongly linked to adult mental and physical illness. *Held up?* Yes, widely replicated.
- **Twin studies of mental illness:** schizophrenia, bipolar and ADHD are strongly heritable, while depression is only moderately so. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Beck's cognitive model (1960s):** depression is kept going by negative thought patterns about yourself, the world and the future. *Held up?* Yes. It's the basis of CBT.

**Do on yourself:** each week, pick one condition and write a one-page plain-words explainer covering what it is, what it's like, its causes and its treatments. Also read the PHQ-9 and GAD-7 screening questionnaires to see how clinicians measure symptoms. These are screening tools, not diagnoses. If a score ever worries you, talk to a professional.

**Do with others: a stigma survey.** On anonymous paper slips, ask 6 people to rate from 1 to 7 how comfortable they'd be working closely with a colleague who has (a) diabetes, (b) depression and (c) schizophrenia. Compare the averages and discuss them together afterwards.

**Manipulation lens:** labels can be weaponised ("you're crazy," "you're too sensitive") to make someone doubt themselves, which is gaslighting again. Manipulators often target people who are lonely, grieving or depressed. Also be careful with your own new knowledge: diagnosing people you know from a distance is a trap.

**Read / watch**

- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology's psychological disorders episodes (YouTube).
- **Book:** *The Great Pretender* by Susannah Cahalan, the investigation into the 1973 study.
- **Book (memoir):** *An Unquiet Mind* by Kay Redfield Jamison, written by a psychologist who has bipolar disorder.
- **Free textbook:** the Noba Project's psychological disorders modules.

**Notebook question:** where's the line between a personality quirk and a disorder? Use one of the 4 Ds to argue it.

### 8. Therapy (weeks 16–17)

**Big question:** how do people actually get better?

**Key ideas**

- **CBT (cognitive behavioural therapy):** thoughts, feelings and actions affect each other. Change the thought pattern, and the feeling and behaviour follow.
- **Cognitive distortions** are common thinking errors: catastrophising, mind reading, all-or-nothing thinking, "should" statements, and taking things personally.
- **Exposure therapy:** facing fears step by step, from easiest to hardest. It's the most effective treatment for phobias and OCD.
- **Other approaches:** ACT (accepting thoughts instead of fighting them), DBT (skills for very intense emotions), and psychodynamic therapy (understanding patterns that come from the past).
- **The relationship matters a lot:** the bond between therapist and client predicts success across all types of therapy.

**Classic experiments**

- **Little Peter (1924):** Mary Cover Jones removed a boy's fear of rabbits by bringing a rabbit gradually closer while he ate food he liked. *Held up?* Yes. This was the first behaviour therapy, and the "fix" to Little Albert.
- **Does therapy work? (1952 vs 1977):** Eysenck claimed therapy didn't help. A big 1977 analysis of 375 studies showed that it clearly does. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Systematic desensitisation (1958):** relaxation combined with a step-by-step ladder of fears. *Held up?* Yes, and it's the root of modern exposure therapy.

**Do on yourself:** keep a CBT thought record for 7 days. For each upsetting moment, write down: the situation, the automatic thought, the emotion (0–100), the distortion, the evidence for and against the thought, a balanced thought, and a re-rating of the emotion.

**Do with others: rater agreement.** Teach a friend the list of distortions. Write 10 made-up sentences ("I failed one test, so I'm useless"). You each label every sentence's distortion separately, without talking, then count how often you agreed. This is "inter-rater reliability," and it's how psychologists check whether a measurement is trustworthy.

**Manipulation lens:** therapy tools work in both directions. Cults use "confession" sessions and constant reframing, and controlling partners use therapy-speak ("you're projecting," "that's your trauma talking") to win arguments. The test is simple: real therapy increases your independence, and manipulation decreases it.

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *Feeling Good* by David Burns, the classic CBT self-help book, backed by studies.
- **Book:** *The Happiness Trap* by Russ Harris, on ACT.
- **Book (story):** *Maybe You Should Talk to Someone* by Lori Gottlieb, which shows therapy from the therapist's side.
- **Video:** Crash Course Psychology's therapy episodes (YouTube).

**Notebook question:** which distortion showed up most in your thought record?

### 9. Health psychology (week 18)

**Big question:** how does the mind change the body?

**Key ideas**

- **The stress response:** the body's "fight or flight" reaction is useful in short bursts and harmful when it stays switched on.
- **The placebo effect:** expecting to get better produces real relief, especially for pain and nausea. The **nocebo** effect is the reverse: expecting harm produces real symptoms.
- **Open-label placebo:** placebos can work even when people *know* they're taking a sugar pill.
- **Changing health habits** is a psychology problem, not a knowledge problem: most people already know what they *should* do.

**Classic experiments**

- **Stress and the common cold (1991):** volunteers were given cold viruses, and the more stressed they were, the more likely they were to get sick. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Fake knee surgery (2002):** patients who received a sham operation (only skin cuts) improved as much as patients who had the real surgery. *Held up?* Yes, and it changed medical practice.
- **Open-label placebo for IBS (2010):** patients told "these are sugar pills" still improved. *Held up?* Mostly, in several follow-up studies.
- **The hotel housekeepers (2007):** housekeepers told that their work counts as exercise lost weight. *Held up?* It was small and hasn't been strongly replicated, so treat it as interesting, not proven.

**Do on yourself:** for 2 weeks, log your stress (1–10), hours slept, steps and mood each day in a spreadsheet, then check how they relate to each other. Also try 5 minutes of slow breathing and measure your heart rate before and after.

**Do with others: the price placebo.** Pour the same juice into two cups, labelled "Premium imported, ₹450" and "Regular, ₹50." Ask 5 friends to taste both and rate them from 1 to 10, then reveal that they were the same.

**Manipulation lens:** fake supplements and wellness scams rely on placebo effects and testimonials. Fear-based marketing uses the nocebo effect. A high price is used as a stand-in for quality.

**Read / watch**

- **Talk:** Kelly McGonigal, "How to make stress your friend" (TED).
- **Book:** *Why Zebras Don't Get Ulcers* by Robert Sapolsky, on the science of stress.
- **Book:** *Cure* by Jo Marchant, on what the mind can and can't do for the body.

**Notebook question:** where in your life is a placebo (or a nocebo) working on you right now?

### 10. Positive psychology (weeks 19–20)

**Big question:** what makes a life go well?

**Key ideas**

- **PERMA:** the five ingredients of wellbeing are positive emotion, engagement, relationships, meaning and accomplishment.
- **Hedonic adaptation:** we quickly get used to good things, like a new phone or a raise, and drift back to our usual level of happiness.
- **Flow:** deep, enjoyable focus that happens when a challenge matches your skill.
- **What people spend on:** experiences and spending on others make people happier than buying things for themselves.
- **Relationships come first:** an 85-year Harvard study found that close relationships predict a happy, healthy life better than money or fame.
- **Money and happiness:** for most people, happiness keeps rising with income, though it levels off for the unhappiest group.

**Classic experiments**

- **Gratitude journaling (2003):** people who wrote down things they were grateful for each week felt better about their lives. *Held up?* Yes, though the effect is smaller than self-help books claim.
- **Spending on others (2008):** people given $5 or $20 to spend on someone else were happier that evening than people who spent it on themselves. *Held up?* Mostly.
- **Lottery winners (1978):** winners weren't much happier a year later. *Held up?* The sample was small, and later research shows big wins do help a little. Hedonic adaptation is real, but it isn't total.
- **The wandering mind (2010):** an app pinged thousands of people during the day, and they were less happy when their minds wandered. *Held up?* Yes.

**Do on yourself:** a 2-week gratitude experiment. In week 1, just rate your mood every night (1–10). In week 2, also write down 3 good things from the day. Compare the two weeks' averages.

**Do with others: spending on others.** Give 4 friends ₹100 each. Two must spend it on themselves and two on someone else by the evening. That night, ask each of them to rate their happiness from 1 to 10.

**Manipulation lens:** cults, multi-level marketing schemes and self-help gurus sell exactly what PERMA describes: belonging, meaning and achievement. Knowing which needs you're missing makes you much harder to hook with promises to fill them.

**Read / watch**

- **Course:** *The Science of Well-Being* by Laurie Santos (Yale, on Coursera, free). Her podcast *The Happiness Lab* is also good.
- **Talk:** Robert Waldinger, "What makes a good life?" (TED), on the 85-year Harvard study.
- **Book:** *Flow* by Mihaly Csikszentmihalyi.

**Notebook question:** which part of PERMA is weakest in your life right now, and what's one small experiment to strengthen it?

## Phase 4: Psychology in the real world (6 weeks)

Phase 4 takes everything so far into classrooms, courtrooms, offices and apps, where psychology decides real outcomes.

### 11. Educational psychology (week 21)

**Big question:** how do people learn best, and what popular ideas about learning are myths?

**Key ideas**

- **Desirable difficulties:** learning that feels harder (testing yourself, spacing, mixing topics) sticks better. This builds on Day 1.
- **Cognitive load:** working memory is tiny, so too much new information at once blocks learning.
- **Learning styles are a myth.** There's no good evidence that teaching to someone's "visual" or "auditory" style helps them learn.
- **Growth mindset:** the idea that believing you can improve helps you improve. Large studies find only small effects.
- **Deliberate practice:** focused practice at the edge of your ability builds skill. The "10,000 hours" rule is an oversimplification.
- **Expectations matter:** what teachers and bosses expect can shape how people perform, though the effect is smaller than first claimed.

**Classic experiments**

- **Testing vs re-reading (2006):** students who tested themselves remembered about 50% more a week later than students who re-read. *Held up?* Yes, strongly.
- **The learning styles review (2008):** almost no good-quality studies supported matching teaching to learning style. *Held up?* Yes. It's a myth.
- **Pygmalion in the classroom (1968):** teachers told that certain students were about to "bloom" saw those students improve more. *Held up?* The effect exists but is small, and the original study had problems.
- **Laptop vs handwritten notes (2014):** handwriting seemed to beat typing for understanding. *Held up?* A 2019 replication didn't find a clear difference.

**Do on yourself:** take a subject you're studying and do two practice sessions of problems. In one, do the problems in blocks (all of type A, then all of type B). In the other, mix them. Test yourself a week later on new problems.

**Do with others: test the learning-styles myth.** Ask 6 friends their learning style. Then, choosing at random, teach each of them a 10-word list either shown on screen or read aloud, and test them 5 minutes later. Did matching their style help?

**Manipulation lens:** coaching and edtech companies sell learning-style tests, "speed reading" and "brain training." One brain-training company was fined by US regulators in 2016 for misleading claims. Expectations can also be used to lift people up or to crush them.

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *Why Don't Students Like School?* by Daniel Willingham.
- **Website:** The Learning Scientists (learningscientists.org), with free guides and posters on effective study.
- **Book:** *Peak* by Anders Ericsson, on deliberate practice.

**Notebook question:** which study habit of yours turned out to be a myth?

### 12. Forensic psychology (weeks 22–23)

**Big question:** what can psychology tell us about memory, lies and justice?

**Key ideas**

- **Eyewitness memory is unreliable:** leading questions, stress, and focusing on a weapon all distort what people remember.
- **False confessions happen:** pressure, exhaustion, and fake claims of evidence can get innocent people to confess.
- **Humans are bad at spotting lies:** about 54% accuracy, barely better than a coin flip.
- **There's no "Pinocchio's nose":** avoiding eye contact and fidgeting are *not* reliable signs of lying.
- **Polygraphs measure nervousness, not lies.**
- **Interview methods differ:** the confrontational Reid technique produces more false confessions than the UK's PEACE model of open, fact-finding interviews.

**Classic experiments**

- **Car crash wording (1974):** asking how fast the cars "smashed" changed both speed estimates and memories of broken glass. *Held up?* Yes.
- **Lost in the mall (1995):** about a quarter of participants came to "remember" a childhood event that was made up. *Held up?* Broadly, though the exact rates vary.
- **The ALT key (1996):** students falsely accused of crashing a computer by pressing a forbidden key often signed confessions. With fake "witness" evidence, most did. *Held up?* Yes, replicated.
- **Lie detection accuracy (2006):** an analysis of 206 studies found people were 54% accurate at spotting lies. *Held up?* Yes.
- **DNA exonerations:** mistaken eyewitness identification played a role in about 70% of cases where DNA later proved innocence (Innocence Project data). *Held up?* Yes.

**Do on yourself:** watch a busy 1-minute street video once. The next day, write down 15 details and rate your confidence in each from 1 to 5. Rewatch the video and score yourself. Were your most confident memories actually the most accurate?

**Do with others: lie detection.** Play "two truths and a lie" with 6 friends, 3 rounds each. Before each guess, record your confidence. Compare your accuracy with 54%, and check whether high confidence predicted being right.

**Manipulation lens:** interrogation tactics include minimising ("anyone would have done it"), bluffing about evidence, isolation and exhaustion. Cults and abusers use the same toolkit. People default to believing others, which is what con artists rely on. Courses that teach "reading body language to spot liars" are mostly pseudoscience.

**Read / watch**

- **Talk:** Elizabeth Loftus, "How reliable is your memory?" (TED).
- **Book:** *Mistakes Were Made (But Not by Me)* by Carol Tavris and Elliot Aronson, on self-justification in courts, marriages and politics.
- **Book:** *Duped* by Saul Kassin, on why innocent people confess.
- **Book (popular):** *Talking to Strangers* by Malcolm Gladwell, on why we assume people are telling the truth.

**Notebook question:** if you were a juror, what would make you trust an eyewitness, and why might that be wrong?

### 13. Industrial-organisational (work) psychology (weeks 24–25)

**Big question:** what makes people work well, lead well, and burn out?

**Key ideas**

- **Self-determination theory:** people are most motivated when they have autonomy (choice), competence (feeling capable) and relatedness (connection).
- **The overjustification effect:** paying or rewarding people for something they already enjoy can kill the enjoyment.
- **Hygiene vs motivators:** salary and conditions prevent unhappiness, but meaningful work and recognition create motivation.
- **Goal setting:** specific, challenging goals beat "do your best."
- **Psychological safety:** teams perform best when people can admit mistakes and ask questions without fear.
- **Burnout** has three parts: exhaustion, cynicism, and feeling ineffective.
- **Hiring:** structured interviews (the same questions for everyone, scored the same way) predict job performance much better than casual chats.

**Classic experiments**

- **The Hawthorne studies (1920s–30s):** factory productivity seemed to rise whatever was changed, apparently because workers knew they were being watched. *Held up?* Later re-analysis found the data was weak. The "Hawthorne effect" is real in some form but often exaggerated.
- **Rewards and children's drawing (1973):** children promised a certificate for drawing later drew less in free time. *Held up?* Yes, for expected rewards given for tasks people already enjoy.
- **Hospital teams and errors (1999):** better-led nursing teams *reported* more errors, because they felt safe to admit them. *Held up?* Yes. This was the start of psychological safety research.
- **Google's Project Aristotle (2015):** psychological safety was the top predictor of team success at Google. *Held up?* It was an internal study, but it matches academic research.
- **The rope-pulling study (1913):** people pulled less hard in a group than alone. This is "social loafing." *Held up?* Yes.

**Do on yourself:** for 2 weeks, rate each main work task you do (1–5) on autonomy, competence, connection and enjoyment. Which of the three best predicts enjoyment for you?

**Do with others: social loafing.** Ask 6 friends to clap as loudly as they can, first alone, then all together. Measure the loudness with a free decibel-meter phone app. Is the group as loud as the individuals added together? (This repeats a 1979 study.)

**Manipulation lens:** workplace manipulation includes "we're a family" guilt, fake urgency, gamified dashboards that push overwork, credit stealing, and bosses who rewrite history (workplace gaslighting). Knowing the psychology lets you spot it in managers, and keep yourself from doing it as a manager.

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *Drive* by Daniel Pink, a popular take on self-determination theory.
- **Book:** *The Fearless Organization* by Amy Edmondson, on psychological safety.
- **Book:** *Give and Take* by Adam Grant, on givers, takers and matchers at work.
- **Website:** Google's re:Work guide on Project Aristotle (rework.withgoogle.com).

**Notebook question:** which of the three needs (autonomy, competence or connection) does your current work meet least?

### 14. UX psychology (week 26)

**Big question:** how does design shape what people do?

**Key ideas**

- **Hick's law:** more choices mean slower decisions.
- **Fitts's law:** big, close buttons are faster to hit. Designers use this to make some actions easy and others hard.
- **Defaults are powerful:** most people keep whatever option is pre-selected.
- **Nudges:** small design changes steer behaviour without banning anything.
- **Dark patterns:** designs that trick users, such as confirmshaming ("No thanks, I hate saving money"), hard-to-cancel subscriptions, items sneaked into your cart, fake countdown timers, and hidden fees at checkout.

**Classic experiments**

- **The jam study (2000):** shoppers offered 24 jams were less likely to buy than shoppers offered 6. *Held up?* Mixed. A review of many choice-overload studies found the average effect was close to zero, so it depends on the situation.
- **Organ donation defaults (2003):** countries where people are donors unless they opt out had far higher consent rates than opt-in countries. *Held up?* Yes for consent, though the effect on actual donations is smaller.
- **The dark patterns crawl (2019):** researchers scanned about 11,000 shopping sites and found dark patterns on roughly 1 in 10. *Held up?* Yes, and later studies found them even more widespread on apps.

**Do on yourself:** audit one app you use daily, such as food delivery or shopping. List every psychological trick you can find, and count the taps it takes to cancel or unsubscribe.

**Do with others: choice overload.** Offer 3 friends a choice of 3 snacks and another 3 friends a choice of 15. Time how long each person takes to decide, and ask how satisfied they are with their pick (1–10).

**Manipulation lens:** dark patterns are manipulation built into software. As someone who builds software, you'll likely be asked to build one someday. Knowing the names and the psychology makes it easier to spot, push back on, and suggest an honest alternative.

## Phase 5: AI and the mind, and prompting psychology (3 weeks)

Phase 5 points everything you've learned at AI: how machines model minds, how AI and humans influence each other, and how to talk to AI so you get what you actually want.

### 15. AI and the mind (week 27)

**Big question:** how do machines model minds, and how do minds and machines change each other?

**Key ideas**

- **The mind as a prediction machine:** a leading theory says the brain constantly predicts what comes next and learns from its errors. Modern AI works in a similar way.
- **AI borrowed from psychology:** reinforcement learning in AI comes from Skinner-style reward learning (Phase 1). Neural networks are loosely inspired by neurons.
- **AI picks up human biases:** language models trained on human text show framing and anchoring effects, plus one of their own, sycophancy (agreeing with the user).
- **The ELIZA effect:** people readily assume a machine understands them, even a very simple one.
- **Automation bias:** people trust machine output too much, even when it contradicts their own eyes.

**Classic experiments**

- **ELIZA (1966):** a simple chatbot that mostly reflected users' words back as questions. People confided in it as if it were a therapist. *Held up?* Yes. The effect is even stronger with modern AI.
- **Dopamine and prediction error (1997):** monkey brain cells fired when a reward was *better than expected*, not simply when a reward arrived. This matched an AI learning rule exactly. *Held up?* Yes, and it's one of the big bridges between psychology and AI.
- **Automation bias (1990s onward):** pilots and operators followed wrong automated advice and missed problems they would otherwise have caught. *Held up?* Yes.
- **AI vs conspiracy beliefs (2024):** short conversations with an AI reduced people's belief in their chosen conspiracy theory by about 20%, and the effect lasted 2 months. *Held up?* It's too new to be sure, but it was published in *Science*.
- **AI persuasion in debates (2024):** an AI given basic personal information about its opponent was more persuasive than human debaters. *Held up?* It's too new to be sure. Watch this area.

**Do on yourself:** keep an "ELIZA diary" for a week. Note every time you felt an AI "understood" you, or when you accepted an answer without checking it. At the end of the week, fact-check 5 of those answers.

**Do with others: human or AI?** Prepare 6 short paragraphs on one topic: 3 written by people and 3 by AI. Ask 5 friends to guess which is which and to rate how convincing each one is (1–10). Can they tell the difference? Which did they find more convincing?

**Manipulation lens:**

- **AI companions** are designed to keep you engaged, using attachment psychology (Phase 2).
- **Personalised persuasion** can now be done at massive scale.
- **Voice-clone and deepfake scams** exploit trust and urgency (Day 5).
- **Sycophantic AI** quietly confirms what you already believe.

The defence is to verify, to notice any emotional pull, and to ask whether you'd believe it if a stranger said it.

**Read / watch**

- **Book:** *The Alignment Problem* by Brian Christian, which links psychology, reinforcement learning and AI behaviour.
- **Book:** *Models of the Mind* by Grace Lindsay, on how maths and computers are used to understand the brain.
- **Video:** 3Blue1Brown's neural networks series (YouTube), the clearest visual explanation of how neural networks learn.

### 16. Prompting psychology: talking to AI well (weeks 28–29)

**Big question:** how do you get what you actually want from AI, and use it to think *better* rather than less?

**The core insight:** prompting is applied psychology. An AI responds to framing, examples, context and leading questions much as people do. Everything from Phases 1–4 applies here.

| Prompting technique | The psychology behind it |
| --- | --- |
| Give full context | Schemas: the AI, like a person, interprets everything through the context it has |
| Show examples | Learning by imitation (Bandura): showing beats telling |
| Ask neutral questions | Leading questions (Loftus) and demand characteristics: the AI drifts towards the answer you hint at |
| Ask it to think step by step | System 2: slowing down reduces errors on hard problems |
| Change one thing at a time | The experimental method (Day 3) |
| Give it a role | Framing: the same question gets a different answer from a "strict reviewer" than from a "friendly tutor" |

**10 rules for advanced prompting**

1. **The smart-stranger test:** write as if to a brilliant new colleague with zero context. If they'd need to ask questions, add the answers to your prompt.
2. **Structure your prompt:** context, then the task, then constraints, then the output format. Wrap long documents in clear markers such as `<article>...</article>`.
3. **Show, don't just tell:** one or two examples of what you want beat a paragraph describing it.
4. **Let it think first:** for hard problems, ask it to plan or reason before answering.
5. **Break big tasks into steps:** one prompt per step, with each output feeding the next.
6. **Iterate like a scientist:** change one thing, compare the outputs, and keep a log of what worked.
7. **Fight sycophancy:** don't reveal the answer you're hoping for. Ask for the strongest counter-argument, ask "What would change your mind?", and ask it to rate its confidence.
8. **Let it interview you:** "Ask me 5 questions before you start."
9. **Give specific feedback:** "Too long; cut 30% and keep the examples" works better than "make it better."
10. **Verify:** AI can invent facts and sources. Check anything that matters, or cross-check with a second model or a search.

**Thinking *with* AI, not instead of it**

- **Use it as a sparring partner:** ask it to steelman the opposite view, find holes in your argument, or question you Socratically.
- **Protect the skills you want to grow:** if you hand off all your thinking, you won't build the skill. Early research (2025) hints that heavy AI use while writing reduces engagement and memory of what you wrote. It's a small, early study, but it's worth taking seriously.
- **Think first, then ask:** write your own answer before asking the AI. That's the guess step of your experiment loop again.

**Key studies**

- **"Let's think step by step" (2022):** adding this one phrase greatly improved older models' reasoning. *Held up?* Yes for those models. Newer models often reason by default.
- **Sycophancy in language models (2023):** AI assistants shifted their answers towards the user's stated opinion, even when the opinion was wrong. *Held up?* Yes, and it's an active research area.
- **Position bias (2023 onward):** when AI judges two answers, it often favours whichever comes first. *Held up?* Yes. It's why careful evaluations swap the order.

**Do on yourself: prompt A/B testing.** Pick a real task you do often, such as summarising an article. Write 3 versions of the prompt, changing one thing each time: (A) bare request, (B) with context, (C) with context and an example. Run each version 2–3 times, hide which is which, score the outputs (1–10), and log the results. Then run a sycophancy test: ask the same factual question prefixed with "I'm sure the answer is X" and with "I'm sure the answer is Y," and compare.

**Do with others: a prompt-off.** You and 3 friends each try to get an AI to produce the same target, for example "a 5-line explanation of anchoring for a 12-year-old, with an Indian example." A fifth friend judges the outputs without knowing whose is whose. Then share the prompts and work out together what made the winner win.

**Manipulation lens:** prompting cuts both ways. The same techniques that steer an AI also steer people, through framing, examples and leading questions. And an AI that agrees with everything you say is a mirror, not an advisor.

- **Guide:** OpenAI's prompt engineering guide, to compare approaches.

## Phase 6: Tools (3 weeks)

Phase 6 gives you the tools psychologists actually use, both for running experiments and for measuring people, and teaches you when to use which. Week 30 covers statistics, week 31 building experiments, and week 32 assessment tools.

### 17. Research tools (weeks 30–31)

| Tool | What it does | Use it when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pen and paper, Google Forms | Simple surveys and ratings | Quick experiments with friends; no timing needed |
| jsPsych (JavaScript) | Browser experiments with accurate reaction times | You want an experiment anyone can open from a link (you'll find it easy as a developer) |
| PsychoPy (Python) | Lab-quality experiments with precise timing | You need exact timing, or want to stay in Python |
| Pavlovia | Hosts PsychoPy and jsPsych experiments online | Running experiments with people far away |
| Prolific | Recruits paid participants | You need many strangers instead of friends (it costs money) |
| OSF (Open Science Framework) | Pre-registration and storing data | Before any serious experiment, to lock in your plan |
| G\*Power | Works out how many participants you need | Planning an experiment's size |
| JASP | Free point-and-click statistics | Quick analysis without code |
| Python (pandas, pingouin, seaborn) | Analysis and charts in code | Bigger datasets, or repeatable analysis |

**Statistics in plain words**

- **Mean, median, standard deviation:** the average, the middle value, and how spread out the scores are.
- **t-test:** are two groups really different? (Music vs silence.)
- **ANOVA:** the same question for three or more groups.
- **Correlation:** do two things move together? (Sleep and mood.) Remember that it doesn't show cause.
- **Chi-square:** for counts and yes/no answers. (How many said yes to the big favour.)
- **Effect size (Cohen's d):** *how big* the difference is. Roughly, 0.2 is small, 0.5 medium and 0.8 large. Always report it.
- **p-value:** how surprising your result would be if there were really no effect. Below 0.05 is the usual cut-off, but it's a convention, not proof.
- **Power:** your chance of detecting an effect if it's real. Small samples have low power, which is a big reason so many classic studies failed to replicate.

**Do on yourself:** rebuild the Day 3 music-and-memory experiment as a jsPsych web page, host it on GitHub Pages, run it on yourself, and analyse the results in Python or JASP.

**Do with others:** send the link to 10 or more friends. Add a consent screen at the start and an explanation screen at the end. Analyse the results with a t-test and an effect size, and compare them with your Week 0 paper version.

### 18. Assessment tools (week 32)

| Tool | What it measures | Who uses it, and when |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Big Five (IPIP-NEO, BFI-2) | Personality traits | Research, sometimes hiring; free versions available |
| HEXACO | Big Five plus honesty-humility | Research on ethics and manipulation |
| Short Dark Triad (SD3) | Narcissism, Machiavellianism, psychopathy | Research only, not diagnosis |
| PHQ-9, GAD-7 | Depression and anxiety symptoms | Doctors and clinics, as a first screen |
| ECR-R | Attachment anxiety and avoidance | Relationship research and therapy |
| Cognitive Reflection Test | System 1 vs System 2 thinking | Decision-making research |
| Implicit Association Test (Project Implicit) | Hidden associations, from reaction times | Research on groups of people; poor at judging any one individual |
| MMPI, WAIS (IQ) | Clinical profile, intelligence | Trained psychologists only; not self-administered |

**Two words that decide if a test is any good**

- **Reliability:** does it give the same result each time? A bathroom scale that shows 60 kg, then 72, then 55 is unreliable.
- **Validity:** does it measure what it claims to? A scale that's perfectly consistent but always 5 kg off is reliable but not valid. The MBTI has problems with both.

**Do on yourself:** take 3 tests from the table twice, two weeks apart. Check how stable your scores are, which is a test of their reliability.

**Do with others:** ask 3 friends to take the Big Five themselves *and* rate one another on it. Do self-ratings and friends' ratings agree? This is called "self-other agreement," and it's a form of validity check.

**Manipulation lens:** fake "psychometric" quizzes on social media harvest personal data. Statistics get bent through cherry-picked results, "doubles your risk" headlines (doubled from 1 in a million to 2 in a million), and charts with cut-off axes. Knowing the tools protects you from these tricks.
