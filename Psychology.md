Some psychology notes:

**What is a psychology experiment?**

You have a guess about how people think, so you set up a small situation, watch what people do, and see whether your guess was right. That's all an experiment is. Here are four famous ones, told as short stories.

**1. Pavlov's dogs (about 1900)**
Pavlov rang a bell every time he fed his dogs. After a while, the dogs started drooling as soon as they heard the bell, even when no food came.
*Lesson:* we learn to connect things that keep happening together. It's why your phone's notification sound gives you a little jolt.

**2. Asch's lines (1950s)**
A student sat in a room with 7 other people and was shown a few lines on a card. The question was simple: "Which line is longest?" Secretly, the other 7 were actors, and they all confidently gave the same wrong answer. About a third of the time, the real student went along with the wrong answer too.
*Lesson:* we often agree with a group even when we can see it's wrong.

**3. Milgram's shocks (1960s)**
People were told to give electric shocks to a stranger whenever he got a question wrong. The shocks were fake and the stranger was an actor, but the participants didn't know that. Most kept going up to the highest level just because a man in a lab coat said "please continue."
*Lesson:* ordinary people will do surprising things when an authority tells them to.

**4. The invisible gorilla (1999)**
People watched a video and counted how many times players passed a basketball. Partway through, a person in a gorilla suit walked right through the middle. About half the viewers never noticed it.
*Lesson:* when we focus hard on one thing, we can miss things right in front of us.

**One warning:** some famous experiments turned out to be wrong. When other scientists repeated them, they didn't get the same result. So a good experimenter always asks, "Did this work again when someone else tried it?"

**Try one yourself **
Write the word **RED** in blue pen, **GREEN** in red pen, and **BLUE** in green pen. Then say out loud the *ink colour*, not the word. You'll find yourself slowing down or slipping up. That's the **Stroop effect**: your brain reads the word automatically, and it gets in the way.

**5. The forgetting curve (Ebbinghaus, 1885)**
Ebbinghaus wanted to know how fast we forget, so he tested himself. He memorised lists of made-up words like "ZOF" and "BIK," then checked how many he still remembered after an hour, a day and a week. He forgot most of them in the first day, and after that the forgetting slowed down. He also found that if he reviewed the words again a few days later, they stayed in his memory much longer.
*Lesson:* we forget fast, but going over something again at spaced-out times makes it stick. That's why cramming the night before a test doesn't last.
*Try it:* Learn these 10 words now: **lamp, river, coin, tiger, shoe, cloud, drum, apple, chair, rope**. Don't look at them again. Tomorrow evening, write down as many as you can remember.

**6. The serial position effect**
Scientists read people a list of about 15 words and asked them to recall as many as they could. Almost everyone remembered the first few words and the last few words best, and forgot the ones in the middle. The first words get more mental practice, and the last words are still fresh when you start recalling.
*Lesson:* beginnings and endings stick; the middle gets lost. It's why speeches and interviews put the important points at the start and the end.
*Try it:* Tomorrow, check which of the 10 words you remembered. You'll probably get "lamp," "river," "apple," "chair" and "rope," and miss some from the middle.

__**7. The car crash and the word "smashed" (Loftus, 1974)**
People watched a video of two cars colliding. Afterwards, one group was asked, "How fast were the cars going when they *hit* each other?" Another group was asked, "How fast were they going when they *smashed* into each other?" The "smashed" group guessed higher speeds. A week later, many of them also said they had seen broken glass in the video, and there wasn't any.
*Lesson:* memory isn't a recording. It can be changed by the words other people use when they ask you about it. That's why the police have to be careful how they question eyewitnesses.
*Try it:* Ask two friends separately about a movie or event you all saw. Ask one, "How annoying was that guy?" and the other, "Was that guy annoying at all?" Notice how their answers differ.__

**The pattern so far**
All seven experiments follow the same recipe: a question about the mind, a small clever setup, watching what people actually do, and a lesson. Once you see that recipe, you can read any experiment and understand it.

In these experiments, real people (or animals) got hurt. They're the reason psychology now has strict safety rules. Same simple style as before, but with no "try it" part, because nobody should repeat these.

**1. Little Albert (1920)**
Watson took a 9-month-old baby called Albert and showed him a white rat. At first Albert liked it and reached for it. Then, every time Albert touched the rat, Watson banged a steel bar loudly behind his head. Soon Albert cried at the rat, and also at rabbits, fur coats and even a Santa Claus mask.
*What it showed:* fear can be learned.
*Why it was wrong:* they made a baby afraid on purpose and never took the fear away.

**2. The Monster Study (1939)**
Researchers in Iowa went to an orphanage. They told some children who spoke perfectly normally that they had a stuttering problem and must be careful when talking. Several of those children became anxious and quiet, and some struggled with speaking for years afterwards.
*What it showed:* labels and criticism can change how a child behaves.
*Why it was wrong:* they deliberately harmed vulnerable children who had nobody to protect them. The university apologised publicly in 2001.

**3. Harlow's monkeys (1950s–60s)**
Harlow took baby monkeys away from their mothers. He gave them two fake "mothers": one made of wire that gave milk, and one covered in soft cloth that gave no food. The babies clung to the cloth mother and only visited the wire one to feed. Later he kept some monkeys completely alone for months, and they came out badly damaged for life.
*What it showed:* babies need comfort and touch, not just food. This changed how orphanages and hospitals treat children.
*Why it was wrong:* the isolation experiments caused extreme suffering.

**4. The Stanford Prison Experiment (1971)**
Zimbardo turned a university basement into a fake prison. Students were randomly made "guards" or "prisoners." Within days, some guards became cruel and some prisoners broke down. It was planned for two weeks and stopped after six days.
*What people believed it showed:* put normal people in a bad role and they turn bad.
*The twist:* later evidence showed the guards were encouraged to be tough, so many experts now say it doesn't prove much at all. It's a lesson in bad science as well as bad ethics.

**5. MKUltra (1950s–60s)**
The CIA, the US spy agency, secretly tested mind-control methods on people. They gave drugs like LSD to people who didn't know they were being drugged, and they used sleep deprivation and other harsh methods. Lives were badly damaged, and at least one man died.
*What it showed:* very little scientifically.
*Why it was wrong:* people were experimented on without being told or agreeing.

**Milgram (from earlier) belongs here too.** Nobody was actually shocked, but many participants left upset, believing they could have killed someone.

**What changed because of all this**
These experiments led to the rules every researcher follows today:
1. **Informed consent:** people must be told what the study involves and must agree.
2. **The right to leave:** anyone can stop at any time.
3. **No lasting harm:** the study can't leave people damaged.
4. **Ethics boards:** a committee must approve a study before it runs.
5. **Debriefing:** if people were misled during the study, they must be told the truth afterwards.

So the next level of learning isn't only "what happened." It's asking, for every experiment, whether it was a good question and whether it was done fairly.
